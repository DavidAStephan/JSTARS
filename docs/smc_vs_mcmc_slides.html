1 · Where we start: Metropolis–Hastings

MH is one hiker mapping a mountain range in fog

The posterior is a landscape over the parameters: high ground = parameter values that explain the data well. We can measure the altitude anywhere we stand, but we can’t see the whole map.

MH sends out one hiker. At each step they propose a small random move; uphill moves are accepted, downhill moves sometimes accepted (so they don’t get stuck on the first bump). Record every position visited — after enough steps, the trail itself traces out the map: more time spent where the posterior is high.

The catch: every step depends on the previous one. The hiker must personally walk to every region that matters, the early wandering must be thrown away (“burn-in”), and consecutive positions are nearly identical — so a million steps can contain only a few thousand steps’ worth of real information. And a walk can’t be parallelised.

2 · The SMC idea

SMC is a crowd, solving a sequence of easier problems

Instead of one hiker on the hard problem, SMC uses thousands of guesses at once (“particles” — we use 2,000) and never asks them to solve the hard problem directly.

It builds a bridge of intermediate problems. Start from the prior — trivially easy, we can draw from it exactly. Then turn the data’s influence up gradually: target ∝ prior × likelihoodφ, with the dial φ moving from 0 (data off) to 1 (the true posterior). Each notch of the dial changes the landscape only slightly — so the crowd only ever has to adapt a little.

Think annealing: you don’t quench hot metal into its final shape in one shock — you cool it in small steps so the structure can keep re-equilibrating. φ is the temperature dial, and the algorithm chooses each notch adaptively: small enough that the crowd never falls badly out of step with the landscape.

3 · One notch of the dial

Each step is the same three moves: weigh, select, explore

1 · Reweight

Score every particle

Turn the dial one notch and ask: how well does each guess explain the extra slice of data-influence just added? Good fits gain weight, poor fits lose it. No one moves yet.

2 · Resample

Clone the fit, drop the unfit

When weights get too uneven, resample: particles are duplicated in proportion to their weight and the rest are discarded. Survival of the fittest — the crowd concentrates where the evidence points.

3 · Mutate

Let everyone take a short walk

Cloning leaves duplicates, so each particle takes a few small MH steps to spread back out and explore locally. This is where MH lives inside SMC — demoted from the whole algorithm to a short refresh.

The division of labour is the key intuition: tempering does the global work (finding the right region happens gradually, as a population), so MH only ever does local work (a few steps to restore diversity). Nobody has to walk across the whole mountain range any more. A bonus: those short walks run independently for every particle — perfectly parallel.

4 · A toy example

Estimating one mean, both ways

The model: five observations y = {1.2, 2.6, 1.5, 2.3, 1.4}, each yi ~ N(θ, 1), and a prior belief θ ~ N(0, 2²). Because this example is conjugate we know the true answer — the posterior is N(1.71, 0.44²) — so we can watch both methods find it.

MH gets there by walking

  1. Start the hiker anywhere, say θ = 0.
  2. Propose a nudge: θ ’ = 0.6. The posterior is higher there (closer to the data) → accept.
  3. Propose θ ’ = 0.1. Posterior is lower → accept only with probability = the height ratio (≈ 8%) → usually reject, stay put.
  4. Repeat ≈ 50,000 times. Throw away the first few thousand (the walk in from θ = 0). The histogram of the trail → N(1.71, 0.44²).

Consecutive positions are nearly identical, so those 50,000 steps hold far fewer independent draws.

SMC gets there by breeding

  1. Draw 1,000 particles directly from the prior N(0, 2²) — an exact sample, no burn-in, spread from −5 to +5.
  2. Turn the dial to φ = ⅓: weight each particle by likelihood⅓. Particles near 1.8 get heavy; a particle at −3 becomes worthless.
  3. Resample: the particle at 2.0 is cloned several times, the one at −3 disappears. A few small MH steps shake the clones apart.
  4. Two more notches (φ = ⅔, then 1) and the cloud itself is N(1.71, 0.44²) — done in a handful of stages.

At every intermediate φ, the cloud is a good sample of that stage’s target — it is never asked to make a leap.

Same destination, opposite journeys: MH accumulates the posterior over time along one path; SMC morphs an entire sample of the prior into a sample of the posterior. On this toy problem both work fine — the differences bite when the landscape has ridges, plateaus and 69 dimensions.

5 · Side by side

The same goal, opposite strategies

MH: one long walkSMC: a guided crowd
Explorers One chain, millions of dependent steps Thousands of particles, tens of stages
How the hard problem is tackled Head-on: the walker faces the full posterior from step one As a bridge: a sequence of slightly-harder problems, prior → posterior
Global vs local The walk must do both — cross between regions and explore within them Tempering + resampling handle global structure; MH steps only polish locally
Burn-in Needed — early steps start in the wrong place and are discarded None — the crowd starts as exact draws from the prior
Parallelism Poor — a walk is inherently one-after-another Natural — every particle’s likelihood and mutation is independent
Extras for free — The marginal likelihood (model evidence) drops out of the reweighting sums
When mixing is poor Run (much) longer More mutation steps, a finer φ bridge, or a better mutation kernel — each targetable separately
One thing does not change: both methods target exactly the same posterior, and both rest on the same accept–reject logic — SMC just wraps it in a schedule that spares any single walker from doing the impossible.
